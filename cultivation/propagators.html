Notes on Seed Pre-treatment and the Hardy Orchid Society Fungal Strains

By John Hagger

The Hardy Orchid Society maintains a collection of fungal strains that are available to members interested in the symbiotic propagation of hardy orchids. Over the years I have been asked many questions about the practical use of these fungi and about seed pre-treatment methods.

The notes below are based on my own experiences, together with feedback received from HOS members who have used these fungi over many years. As with all aspects of orchid propagation, there are variables that can affect outcomes, so experimentation and careful records remain essential.

Seed Pre-treatment

Successful germination of hardy orchid seed depends at least as much on effective pre-treatment as on the fungal strain itself. Optimum treatments have not been determined for most species, so repeated trials are usually needed.

Sterilisation

Most growers use either sodium hypochlorite or calcium hypochlorite.

  • Sodium hypochlorite – The author’s preferred method is a 10% solution of unscented Domestos Original (a UK household bleach containing approximately 4.5% NaOCl).
  • Calcium hypochlorite – Usually used as a saturated solution.

Bleaching times are commonly judged by the “half-bleach” appearance of the seed, but concentration, formulation (thin vs thick bleach) and additives all influence the outcome. There is no single protocol that works equally for all species and seed lots.

Wetting the Seed

Hardy orchid seed is naturally hydrophobic and tends to float. Seed that sinks is easier to sterilise evenly.

A brief pre-soak with a wetting agent helps. Tween-80 is frequently recommended; a single drop of unscented Fairy Liquid Original (washing-up liquid) is a readily available alternative used by some growers.

The possible toxicity of surfactants, colourants or thickeners in domestic products is unknown, but in practice these additives have not been observed to prevent germination when seed quality and treatment are otherwise appropriate.

Other Treatments

  • Plant growth regulators – Some growers report improved results with kinetin (for certain Cypripedium species) or gibberellic acid (for some Orchis species).
  • Scarification – Mechanical scarification (e.g. with sand and a power drill) can improve germination of older seed or species with thick coats.
  • Activated charcoal – Generally beneficial in asymbiotic media, but it has been shown to inhibit germination when added to Basic Oats Medium and is therefore best avoided in that context.

Fungi

Quick guide: B1 and A36 (Ceratobasidium) for most Dactylorhiza & Anacamptis; Q414 and AMYG (Tulasnella) for Serapias.

The Hardy Orchid Society currently maintains five fungal strains that have been used successfully for the symbiotic propagation of hardy orchids. Although all have value, they should not be regarded as universally suitable.

B1

B1 is a strain of Ceratobasidium originally isolated from the roots of a Dactylorhiza species by the late Jim Hill during the 1990s. It was the first fungal strain to become widely distributed within the Society.

If restricted to a single fungal strain, B1 would often be the first choice. On Basic Oats Medium it reliably germinates many species and hybrids of Dactylorhiza together with most Anacamptis.

However, it is not universally successful. Some species, including Anacamptis pyramidalis, can be difficult with B1.

A36

A36 is another strain of Ceratobasidium, isolated some years later by the late Adrian Blundell. Whilst generally less vigorous than B1, it germinates a broadly similar range of species.

Some orchids appear to show a clear preference for A36. In particular, some diploid Dactylorhiza forms have responded strongly, and successful germination of Anacamptis papilionacea has been achieved where B1 failed.

AP6

AP6 is the third Ceratobasidium strain maintained by the Society. It was recovered from historic material donated by Richard Manuel, although the identity of the original host species is uncertain.

AP6 is notably vigorous. That vigour may occasionally limit usefulness, as rapid fungal overgrowth can outpace protocorm development in some sowings.

Q414

Q414 is a strain of Tulasnella and represents the only surviving fungal isolate from conservation work undertaken at Kew during the late 1980s.

Compared with Ceratobasidium strains, Q414 grows slowly and can appear weak in culture. Despite this, it remains highly valuable for species that do not respond well to the Society’s Ceratobasidium isolates.

AMYG

AMYG is the second Tulasnella strain maintained by the Society and was also recovered from historic material supplied by Richard Manuel.

Of the two Tulasnella strains, AMYG is often preferred because it grows more vigorously and has produced strong results in repeated sowings, especially with Serapias species.

New Approaches and Future Developments

Most observations above relate to the traditional symbiotic method using Basic Oats Medium. Interest in alternative methods has increased considerably in recent years, including cardboard-based systems and other low-cost techniques.

There has also been interest in using Ilionectria destructans (syn. I. radicicola) on synthetic autoclaved soil media for difficult species.

Important note: Both the Hardy Orchid Society Conservation Officer and the Animal and Plant Health Agency advise against use of this fungus because of its recognised pathogenic potential.

Encouragingly, recent observations suggest that some difficult species may also be germinated using existing HOS strains, and ongoing trials continue to refine practical guidance.
