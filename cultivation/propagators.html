Information for Propagators

Notes on Seed Pre-treatment and the Hardy Orchid Society Fungal Strains
By John Hagger

The Hardy Orchid Society maintains a collection of fungal strains that are available to members interested in the symbiotic propagation of hardy orchids. Over the years I have been asked many questions about these fungi, seed sterilisation and pre-treatment, and which species can successfully be propagated using the various strains.

The notes below are based on my own experiences, together with feedback received from HOS members who have used these fungi over many years. As with all aspects of orchid propagation, there are very few absolute rules. Results can vary considerably depending upon the age and quality of the seed, the effectiveness of sterilisation, the growing medium used and the fungal strain selected. What works well in one laboratory or growing area may not always produce identical results elsewhere.

Seed Pre-treatment

Successful germination of hardy orchid seed depends at least as much on effective pre-treatment as on the fungal strain itself. Optimum treatments have not been determined for most species, so results can vary with seed age, seed-coat thickness and the particular product used.

Sterilisation

Most growers use either sodium hypochlorite or calcium hypochlorite.

  • Sodium hypochlorite – The author’s preferred method is a 10% solution of unscented Domestos Original (a UK household bleach containing approximately 4.5% NaOCl). This gives a working strength of roughly 0.5% sodium hypochlorite and provides good batch-to-batch consistency.
  • Calcium hypochlorite – Usually used as a saturated solution.

Bleaching times are commonly judged by the “half-bleach” appearance of the seed, but concentration, formulation (thin vs thick bleach) and additives all influence the outcome. There is no single fixed protocol that works for every species.

Wetting the Seed

Hardy orchid seed is naturally hydrophobic and tends to float. Seed that sinks is easier to sterilise evenly.

A brief pre-soak with a wetting agent helps. Tween-80 is frequently recommended; a single drop of unscented Fairy Liquid Original (washing-up liquid) is a readily available alternative used by the author.

The possible toxicity of surfactants, colourants or thickeners in domestic products is unknown, but in practice these additives have not been observed to prevent germination when seed quality and sterilisation are otherwise good.

Other Treatments

  • Plant growth regulators – Some growers report improved results with kinetin (for certain Cypripedium species) or gibberellic acid (for some Orchis species) either as a pre-soak or in the medium. Their value in symbiotic culture on Basic Oats Medium remains unproven.
  • Scarification – Mechanical scarification (e.g. with sand and a power drill) can improve germination of older seed or species with thick coats.
  • Activated charcoal – Generally beneficial in asymbiotic media, but it has been shown to inhibit germination when added to Basic Oats Medium and is therefore best avoided in routine symbiotic work.

Fungi

The Hardy Orchid Society currently maintains five fungal strains that have been used successfully for the symbiotic propagation of hardy orchids. Although all have value, they should not be regarded as interchangeable. Different orchid species often respond differently to individual fungal strains and, in some cases, closely related orchids appear to show distinct preferences.

B1

B1 is a strain of Ceratobasidium originally isolated from the roots of a Dactylorhiza species by the late Jim Hill during the 1990s. It was the first fungal strain to become widely distributed within the Society and remains one of the most useful and most widely used strains available.

If I were restricted to a single fungal strain, B1 would probably be my first choice. On Basic Oats Medium it reliably germinates all species and hybrids of Dactylorhiza together with most species of Anacamptis. It has also been used successfully with Bletilla, Spiranthes, Neotinea maculata and, according to some reports, certain species of Platanthera.

However, it is not universally successful. I have never managed to germinate Anacamptis pyramidalis using B1 and have also failed repeatedly with Anacamptis papilionacea. Some growers report successful germination of Gymnadenia conopsea, although I have never achieved reliable results myself.

Species possessing relatively thick seed coats, such as Anacamptis palustris, often appear particularly sensitive to seed age and the effectiveness of pre-treatment. In such circumstances poor germination should not automatically be regarded as evidence that the fungus itself is unsuitable.

For tetraploid Dactylorhiza species, B1 frequently appears to be the most effective fungal strain maintained by the Society.

A36

A36 is another strain of Ceratobasidium, isolated some years later by the late Adrian Blundell. Whilst generally less vigorous than B1, it germinates a broadly similar range of orchid species.

Over the years I have gained the strong impression that certain orchids show a clear preference for A36. In particular, diploid Dactylorhiza species, especially forms of Dactylorhiza incarnata, often seem to germinate more readily with A36 than with B1. In contrast, many tetraploid Dactylorhiza species appear to favour B1.

A36 has also enabled me to achieve successful germination of Anacamptis papilionacea, a species that consistently failed when sown with B1.

I have also succeeded in germinating Gymnadenia species with A36. However, whilst germination may be achieved, growing the resulting protocorms into robust seedlings has proved considerably more difficult. Despite repeated attempts, I have never succeeded in raising them to weanling size using the traditional symbiotic method on Basic Oats Medium.

AP6

AP6 is the third Ceratobasidium strain maintained by the Society. It was successfully recovered from historic material donated by Richard Manuel, although the identity of the original host species can no longer be determined with certainty. It may have originated from either Anacamptis papilionacea or Anacamptis pyramidalis.

AP6 is the most vigorous-growing Ceratobasidium strain currently maintained by the Society and appears genetically very close to a fungal isolate obtained from the roots of Anacamptis morio.

Relatively few members have extensive experience using AP6 and consequently much remains to be learned regarding its capabilities. I have successfully used it to germinate Dactylorhiza praetermissa and Dactylorhiza fuchsii.

One characteristic that may limit its usefulness is its exceptional vigour. On several occasions I have observed cultures germinating successfully before the developing protocorms appeared to become overwhelmed by fungal growth. Whether this is directly attributable to the vigour of the strain remains uncertain, but it is a possibility that growers should bear in mind.

Q414

Q414 is a strain of Tulasnella and represents the only surviving fungal isolate from the conservation work undertaken at Kew during the late 1980s. It was subsequently supplied to the late Norman Heywood, who used it extensively in the production of orchids for his nursery, Hardy Orchids. For a period it was also known as “fungus T&M” following its use by Thompson & Morgan.

Compared with the various Ceratobasidium strains, Q414 grows slowly and often appears relatively weak in culture. Nevertheless, it remains an extremely valuable fungus because certain orchid genera appear to respond much more favourably to Tulasnella than to Ceratobasidium.

In my own work, Q414 has proved particularly useful for the germination of Serapias species. These have generally failed to germinate successfully using any of the HOS Ceratobasidium strains on Basic Oats Medium. Q414 has also shown value with at least some species of Spiranthes.

AMYG

AMYG is the second Tulasnella strain maintained by the Society. Like AP6, it was successfully recovered from historic material supplied by Richard Manuel. Unfortunately, neither the origin of the fungus nor the significance of its name can now be recalled.

Of the two Tulasnella strains, AMYG is generally my preferred choice. It grows more vigorously than Q414 and has consistently produced stronger overall results whenever I have selected a Tulasnella fungus.

It has proved particularly valuable for the propagation of Serapias species and would normally be my first choice whenever I believe a Tulasnella fungus may be beneficial.

New Approaches and Future Developments

Most of the observations described above relate to the traditional symbiotic method using Basic Oats Medium. However, interest in alternative propagation methods has increased considerably in recent years.

Of particular interest are the cardboard-based techniques that have become increasingly popular amongst growers. These methods are often less predictable than traditional oat cultures but may provide conditions under which orchid fungi behave very differently. The subject has been discussed in detail by Fraser Anderson in the Journal of the Orchid Society of Great Britain and Ireland.

I have never been convinced that Ceratobasidium and Tulasnella coexist successfully on Basic Oats Medium. In most cases the Ceratobasidium appears to outgrow and overwhelm the Tulasnella. However, observations from cardboard-based systems suggest that the relationship between these fungi may be more complex than previously believed.

One HOS member has reported successful germination of Neotinea ustulata using a cardboard and soil mixture inoculated with all five HOS fungal strains, an outcome that would once have seemed highly unlikely.

There has also been considerable interest in the use of Ilionectria destructans (syn. I. radicicola) on synthetic autoclaved soil media. Reports suggest that this technique may permit germination of species that have traditionally been regarded as extremely difficult or impossible to germinate symbiotically.

Important note: Both the Hardy Orchid Society Conservation Officer and the Animal and Plant Health Agency advise against the use of this fungus because of its recognised pathogenicity to important horticultural and agricultural crops.

Encouragingly, recent observations suggest that some of these difficult species may also be germinated using existing HOS fungal strains. Preliminary results indicate that B1 and Q414 may successfully germinate species such as Orchis purpurea and Orchis militaris, although further work is required to confirm these observations.
