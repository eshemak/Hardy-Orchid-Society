The ‘cardboard method’ of growing orchids from seed

Fraser Anderson
Photos by Fraser Anderson
Originally published in Journal of the Orchid Society of Great Britain, 2025, 74(2)

Orchid seeds are well known for being finicky to germinate. They are usually more or less ‘embryos in packets’ without any reserves of nutrients for germination under their own steam. There are certain exceptions, for example several species of Disa and Bletilla can germinate directly on their own, but by and large, in the wild orchid seeds need to make a partnership with a fungus. The hyphae of these fungi penetrate into the seed and form links with the embryo, providing it with the energy and nutrients to grow.

The most common germination method nowadays is in vitro, in sterile conditions on agar-based media. While this is certainly effective, it can be difficult or cost-prohibitive for the beginner or the amateur to carry out and needs a good deal of research and preparation. The sterile sowing technique removes the need for the fungal partnership by providing the seeds with an abundance of freely available nutrients, which would have been offered by fungal partners. These nutrients are an attractive prospect to contaminant microbes such as bacteria, moulds and yeasts, all of which also readily digest orchid embryos and seeds, so the need to exclude these is paramount in the sterile technique.

However, an alternative, non-sterile technique, the ‘cardboard method’, relies on first growing a healthy culture of specific partner (mycorrhizal) fungi, onto which you sow orchid seeds and establish the link as in nature. This technique is arguably far easier than lab protocols for the sterile technique, and it is certainly much cheaper!

The cardboard technique was first developed by Japanese growers who noticed that cardboard appeared to be a preternaturally good food source for the mycorrhizal fungi that naturally associate with orchids. I am unsure of why this is exactly, but I would imagine that the fungi simply find a board of highly processed, cooked wood pulp to be a readily digestible meal. Additionally, when using in-vitro techniques fungi can also be used and they grow best when a finely balanced availability of nutrients is achieved, typically with the inclusion of oats in the agar. I suspect that cardboard serendipitously achieves a near-perfect ratio of nutrients in order for the fungi to thrive.

There are numerous types of mycorrhizal association among orchids and their partner fungi. This method is suitable only for those fungi species which are at least partially saprotrophic, i.e. those that feed on dead organic matter (such as cardboard). Most orchid mycorrhizal fungi fall into this category, being members of the genera Ceratobasidium, Tulasnella, Sebacina and related groups, or other Basidiomycetes. The associations are vast. Epipactis, for example, has mycorrhizal association with over 100 fungi taxa. The South American genus Codonorchis is also known to have many associates, whereas Chloraea, another genus from the same region, can be extremely specific. Some orchids, for example Orchis purpurea, still have not had a germinating partner fungus identified.

The good news is that several fungi have been isolated and are available to growers (including through the Hardy Orchid Society). The cardboard method works particularly well with these known isolates.

Stage 1: Substrate

My personal recipe consists of equal parts by dry weight of:

  1. Finely shredded/chopped, soaked cardboard (making sure to remove any tape, tape residue, or dyes). I find that cutting the cardboard into ~1 cm squares, then moistening at a rate of 30 ml water to 100 g cardboard before blending will create a fine, fluffy texture that is excellent for a uniform substrate texture. The larger surface area also helps the fungus colonise rapidly.
  2. A coarser component such as chopped coconut coir, fine bark, or a small amount of loam/soil to provide structure and some additional minerals.

The mixture is moistened to a consistency that is damp but not dripping wet. Pasteurisation is useful to reduce unwanted intruders such as soil mites. The substrate can be heated (for example in a microwave or oven) to a temperature that kills mites and many contaminant fungi while leaving the later-inoculated orchid fungus able to colonise.

Containers

Clear plastic containers with lids work well. The lid helps reduce evaporation while still allowing some gaseous exchange and helps prevent intrusion of contaminant moulds, fungus gnats or springtails (which will quickly decimate orchid protocorms). Aim for at least 3–5 cm depth of substrate and ensure it is evenly moist.

Stage 2: Inoculation

Once the containers are prepared and filled, inoculate with a fungus of your choice. You can either use a named, isolated strain (such as the HOS strains B1, A36, Q414, AMYG, etc.) or collect a sample in situ from the roots of a suitable orchid.

There are several methods:

  • Agar scrapings from a pure culture plate
  • Biopsied root sections
  • Swabs from roots

For agar scrapings or root sections, place them directly onto or into the substrate. For swabs, cut off the swab head, moisten it, and add it to the container. Ensure no invertebrates are included. Fungus samples collected outdoors can be frozen for an hour or so to kill invertebrates while usually allowing the fungus itself to survive.

Place the inoculated containers somewhere dark and out of extremes of heat or cold. After a few days the fungus should begin to colonise the cardboard substrate, often producing a visible white or creamy mycelial growth that can look a little like blue cheese in some cases.

Stage 3: Sowing the seed

Once the fungus has thoroughly colonised the substrate, surface-sow the orchid seed. A light dusting is usually sufficient. Do not bury the seed. Replace the lid and return the container to darkness.

Terrestrial temperate orchids usually take around three months to form visible protocorms. Some, such as certain Himantoglossum species, can take up to 18 months. The key is not to give up, and with terrestrial orchids keep the cultures in darkness.

The containers can be left in a cupboard or drawer away from direct heat, or kept in a frost-free location outside (still in darkness). Tropical epiphytes obviously require warm conditions. Some genera such as Cypripedium, certain Orchis species and some other temperate orchids also require vernalisation / cold stratification: keep the germinated protocorms in the fridge or under cold conditions for 5–7 weeks before they will exit the protocorm stage or even germinate.

From protocorm to seedling

As the protocorms enlarge they begin to produce a sprout at one end. When the sprout becomes about two to three times the length of the protocorm – or when it starts to produce chlorophyll (yellowish or greenish colour appearing in the cream-white tissue) – you have two options:

  1. Remove the lid, top-dress the protocorms with a finely sifted seedling compost or sand, and place the container in very diffuse light. The sprouts will push through the top-dressing.
  2. Prick the seedlings out and pot them individually once they have some chlorophyll and preferably some true root formation. This is the author’s preferred method.

Increase light levels gradually and never allow the container to dry out. Once the young plants have a few leaves and look robust they can be potted on and grown in the normal way.

Reference

Jacquemyn H, Waud M, Lievens B, Brys R. (2016). Differences in mycorrhizal communities between Epipactis palustris, Epipactis helleborine and its presumed sister species Epipactis neerlandica. Annals of Botany 118(1): 105–114.
